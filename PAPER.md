## I. Prefatory

Two instruments now occupy the same benches: large-scale learning machines, and devices that prepare and measure quantum states. A third, older labour — the description of living matter — is being rewritten by both.

The present work treats that conjunction under a single name, *world mechanics*. The name is not an ornament. It states a task: to use computation in the service of physical description, and to refuse two cheap errors. The first error is to take fluent speech for a mind. The second is to take the absence of a mind for the absence of a tool.

The standpoint is that of a platform engineer who works with artificial intelligence: the trade of making systems runnable, observable, and repeatable. No academic chair is claimed.

The assumptions are few, and they are physical.

1. There is one world.
2. A scientific sentence is one that can, in principle, meet a measurement.
3. Feeling in animals is a biological fact, not a warrant for attributing feeling to software.
4. No second substance is required.

## II. The language of nature and the language of machines

Human intelligence addresses the world in several dialects: ordinary speech, mathematics, and experiment. Physics is the dialect that has been compelled, for some four centuries, to survive contact with apparatus. Quantum theory is that dialect at small scales. It speaks of amplitudes, operators, decoherence, and the statistics of outcomes.

Artificial intelligence, as it stands in 2026, is not another dialect of nature. It is an instrument. A model is a parameterised function trained to predict, generate, or control. When words are addressed to a language model, a second person is not thereby entered. A next-token machine is steered. The steering can still be useful. It can retrieve, compress, and recombine descriptions already written by human workers. In narrower systems it can propose circuits, trial wave-functions, or molecular candidates. Those proposals remain on probation until theory or experiment returns a number.

The central sentence of this inquiry is therefore simple:

> A machine may model, describe, predict, and simulate aspects of the world. Description is not to be confused with consciousness.

## III. On speaking with a model

A large language model assigns probabilities to tokens given a context. The public face of this process resembles conversation. The mechanism does not.

| Common speech | More exact account |
|---|---|
| It understands | It produces outputs that match competent use within a family of tasks. |
| It thinks | It performs multi-step computation in weights, activations, and often in a scratch of generated tokens. |
| It feels | No evidenced affective system: no body, no interoception, no valence circuitry of the animal kind. |
| Human intelligence was derived from it | False. Nervous systems evolved. Present machines were trained on traces of human work. |

The last row must be kept. Human intelligence is not an offspring of these systems. The systems can nevertheless be studied. Mechanistic interpretability attempts to read internal features and circuits. Work in 2026 has described a privileged set of verbalizable representations — states a model can report and manipulate amid a larger volume of automatic processing. That is a claim about access and control. It is not a claim about what it is like to be the model. Authors who test functional predictions of consciousness theories on synthetic agents have been explicit: the agents are reference implementations, not conscious beings.

The useful asymmetry is therefore this:

- Human intelligence is not derived from artificial intelligence.
- Artificial intelligence can be understood as an engineered object.
- Human intelligence can be understood, in part, as a physical and computational system, without a pretence that the maps are finished.
- What the machine lacks is not a soul. It lacks, at the least, the organisation that makes sensation and emotion possible in animals. Whether anything further is required for experience is an open scientific question. Present models are not known to satisfy those conditions.

## IV. Artificial intelligence in the service of quantum description

The difficulty of many-body quantum systems is representation. The space of states grows without mercy as particles are added. Experiment yields partial views. Theory writes Hamiltonians that often cannot be solved.

Recent work falls into several overlapping methods: learning procedures with stated guarantees; deep networks used as trial wave-functions; and generative models that propose circuits and protocols.

### IV.1 Character and reconstruction

A technical review of 2026 frames two tasks: the prediction of properties of scalable quantum systems, and the reconstruction of approximate descriptions from data. Efficient methods have been given for linear properties and for the classification of phases. The network is not the state in any mystical sense. It is a compressed surrogate that can be queried.

Neural representations of many-body wave-functions have been trained from probability densities and probability currents, then used to pre-train harder interacting problems, including states of the fractional quantum Hall kind. Overlaps reported on the tested cases are high. The questions that remain are scale, physical bias in the architecture, and a candid account of failure.

### IV.2 Circuits for molecules

Electronic structure is the practical bridge to the life sciences. Variational algorithms spend much of their cost inventing a circuit that prepares an approximate ground state. Work reported in 2026, from groups including Quantinuum, NVIDIA, and Pfizer, trains generative models — transformer-like generators with reinforcement learning — to propose such circuits. Large reductions in generation time relative to ADAPT-VQE are claimed on benchmarks. Generated circuits have been executed on commercial hardware for a pharmaceutical molecule, imipramine.

If the claims survive wider scrutiny, the pattern is the pattern of this memoir: the machine searches a discrete space of quantum programmes; physics still decides whether the energy is right.

### IV.3 Verification

Language models invent. Scientific code must not. Benchmarks that extract research-level many-body tasks from the journals show that unaided models fail often. Frameworks that force a programming check and a physics check improve the rate of honest work. Workflows that separate the extraction of theory, the writing of a specification, and the writing of code raise success on methods such as the density-matrix renormalisation group, compared with a single ungoverned request to write the programme.

The posture is that of a laboratory: treat the model as a junior theorist, and put conservation, symmetry, and numerical test between the junior and the archive.

### IV.4 Generative advantage

A distinct claim is computational advantage: families of generative quantum models that are hard to simulate classically, that can be trained without the usual barren landscape, and that have been shown on large superconducting processors both for the learning of intractable distributions and for the learning of circuits that hasten a physical simulation. This is a result about sampling and hardware. It is not a result about mind.

## V. Living matter: molecules, cells, and restraint

In the life sciences, structure prediction is now an ordinary input. Generative models propose backbones and sequences. Multi-agent systems draft hypotheses and plans of analysis. Models of genomic sequence exist at large scale. The industrial story is one of compute and of platforms, not of a single enchanted molecule.

A perspective of 2026 in *Cell* lists fifteen difficulties for generative methods at the scale of the cell. Success at the molecule does not become, by courtesy, a reliable model of tissues and disease. Data are sparse against the combinatorics of cell states. Many phenotypes are multicellular. That list is a map of what is missing.

The honest join between quantum theory and biology is not a doctrine of quantum consciousness. It is two questions.

1. *Quantum for biology.* Can quantum devices, or methods inspired by them, simulate molecules and materials that matter to drugs and enzymes?
2. *Quantum in biology.* Where, if anywhere, do living systems make use of effects that have no adequate classical substitute?

An evidence map of 2026 is careful. The most mature cases of the second kind remain hydrogen tunnelling in some enzymatic transfers, and radical-pair spin chemistry as a serious account of magnetoreception. Photosynthetic complexes show ultrafast oscillations that can be modelled with coherent or vibronically mixed excitons. Whether those signals are functionally long-lived electronic coherences under physiological conditions is still argued, because vibration and ensemble averaging can mimic the same marks.

Warm and wet tissue is a hostile lodging for delicate superposition. Sometimes quantum kinetics still matters. Sometimes a classical story is enough. The experiment decides.

## VI. A scheme of five levels

This is a working map. It is not a new physical theory.

**0. World.** One spacetime; fields or their effective descendants; chemistry; organisms. No second world.

**1. Description.** Hamiltonians, rate equations, statistical models, diagrams. These objects earn their keep by predicting measurements.

**2. Instrument.** Telescopes, sequencers, quantum processors, neural networks, build pipelines. Instruments transform data and search the space of descriptions. They are not additional substances.

**3. Interface language.** Speech, code, circuit diagrams, laboratory protocol. This is how human workers steer instruments and how instruments return results. Fluency here is easily mistaken for a person.

**4. Understanding.** A system understands a domain, in the modest sense used here, when it can be used to generate checkable predictions and interventions, and when its failures can be localised. Human understanding ordinarily includes feeling, care, and a body. Machine understanding in 2026 is the first half at best.

The map forbids animism of the model and dismissal of the model. Both are idle.

## VII. The engineer's condition of repeatability

A model that cannot be rebuilt is not an instrument. The same is true of a circuit generator or of an agent that writes scientific code.

The cycle is ordinary:

Build → Deploy → Observe → Test → Learn → Repeat

Minimum conditions for a result in this programme:

1. A pinned model, image digest, or commit.
2. A command that runs the check again.
3. A log of the number obtained.
4. A note of disagreement with a known formula, paper, or measurement.

That is the contribution of platform engineering to the present inquiry.

Questions that remain in view:

- How far can a machine describe physical reality?
- Where does prediction end and understanding begin?
- How may computation assist quantum and biological research without a confusion of categories?
- What are the practical limits of machine reasoning?
- How should an engineer distinguish capacity from cognition?

## VIII. Limits

The empirical statements cited below belong to their authors. The framing — the name *world mechanics*, the five levels, the insistence on the human/machine asymmetry — is the contribution of this draft.

A preprint on a personal site, or on arXiv, is a public draft. arXiv is commonly free to read and to submit; it is not peer review. A first submission to a category often requires an endorser. LaTeX is preferred.

There is no evidence that present systems have emotions. To treat them as if they did confuses users and cheapens the moral standing of animals, who suffer.

Quantum mechanics is strange enough without the loan of souls. Superposition is a fact about amplitudes. It is not a metaphor for personality.

## Appendix. Glossary

**Amplitude.** A complex number whose squared modulus is a measurement probability in the ordinary quantum recipe.
**Decoherence.** The loss of usable coherence by coupling to an environment.
**Ansatz.** A restricted family of states or circuits, afterwards optimised.
**VQE / ADAPT-VQE.** Variational methods that prepare an approximate ground state.
**Radical pair.** Two correlated electron spins whose chemistry may be sensitive to a magnetic field.
**Tunnelling.** Passage through a barrier forbidden to a classical particle; relevant to some hydrogen transfers in enzymes.
**Token.** A discrete piece of text predicted by a language model.
**Mechanistic interpretability.** The attempt to read the internal features and circuits of a trained model.
**Preprint.** A public draft that has not been journal-reviewed as a condition of posting.
